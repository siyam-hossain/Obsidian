# Why Engineers dislike tightly coupling

Imagine your phone can only charge with one specific charger, made by one specific company. If the charger breaks, you have to buy the exact same model. If you travel and forget it, you can't borrow a charger from anyone else. And if the company stops making that charger, your phone becomes almost useless.

Would you like to use a phone like that?
Probably not.

Hello every one this is Siyam id ends with 065. Today I stand before you to explain a phenomena referred as tightly coupling.

Whereas, I recently share a situation where a phone is fully dependent on a specific charger, right? This type of situation widely know as tightly coupling.

Now, imagine a different phone. It charges with a USB-C cable. If your charger breaks, you can buy another one from almost any brand. If you're at a friend's house, you can borrow their charger. Your phone still works because it doesn't depend on one specific charger rather it depends on the USB-C interface. This situation is called loosely coupling.

In a formal definition,
Tightly coupling means one part of a program depends heavily on another specific part. If one changes occur, then the other often needs to change as well.

On the other hand, loosely coupling means different parts communicate through a common interface or standard, just like USB-C. One part can change without breaking the others.

So, why engineers dislike tightly coupling?
The answer is simple.
Because

- software changes all time.
- customers request new features
- bugs need to be fixed.
- businesses update their requirements.
- Technology keeps improving
  If the software is tightly coupled, even a small change can create problems in many other palaces.

Think about a car, if one wheel flat do you replace the entire car?
Of course not. you replace the flat wheel because it was designed to be independent from the the rest of the car. Good software is built the same way. Each part can be improved or replaced without affecting everything else.

So, the next time someone asks what tightly coupling is, remember your phone charger. If it works with only a special charger then it is tightly coupling and if it works with USB-C charger that's loosely coupling.

# **How Online Privacy Tools Work**

Imagine you send a postcard to your friend.

As the postcard travels, the mail carrier, people at the post office, and anyone who handles it can read your message because it's written openly.

Now imagine instead that you put the message inside a locked box. Only your friend has the key to open it. Everyone can still see that you're sending a box, but no one can read what's inside.

Which method would you choose?

Probably the locked box.

Hello everyone. This is Siyam, ID ends with 065. Today, I stand before you to explain how online privacy tools work.

The example I just shared is very similar to what happens on the internet.

Whenever we browse websites, send messages, or shop online, our information travels across many computers and networks before reaching its destination. Without protection, others may be able to see or collect that information.

This is where online privacy tools come in.

These tools help protect our personal information so that only the right people can access it.

One of the most common privacy tools is encryption.

Encryption works like the locked box in our example. It changes your information into a secret code before sending it over the internet. Only the intended receiver has the key to turn it back into the original message.

Another popular privacy tool is a VPN, or Virtual Private Network.

Think of a VPN as a private tunnel.

Normally, your internet traffic travels on an open road where many people can see where you're going. A VPN creates a secure tunnel, making it much harder for others to watch your online activity.

Privacy tools also include password managers.

Instead of remembering dozens of passwords, a password manager stores them securely and helps you create strong, unique passwords for every account.

Finally, many websites now offer two-factor authentication, or 2FA.

It's like having two locks on your front door. Even if someone steals your password, they still need a second code—usually sent to your phone—to log in.

So, why do online privacy tools matter?

Because today, we use the internet for almost everything—banking, shopping, studying, and communicating.

Without proper protection, our personal information can be stolen, misused, or tracked.

Online privacy tools don't make us invisible, but they make it much harder for others to access our data without permission.

So, the next time you browse the internet, remember the locked box.

Just as you wouldn't send your private letters for everyone to read, you shouldn't send your digital information without protecting it.
